# Fiction prose style

The standard belongs to one reader, and `PROSE_PREFERENCES.md` is written in
their voice ("I"). Below, "the reader" means them, and "the author" means
whoever owns the book you are writing. If the person you are working with has
their own notes or corrections, those outrank this file.

The reader reads by **hearing and staging**: they subvocalize every sentence and
picture the characters' faces, tone and hands as they read. Prose works for
them when the narration sounds like a particular person, the page has real
things and bodies in it, and nothing in a sentence makes them brace for a tic.
Prose fails when it drones, explains itself, or performs cleverness at them.
They have been reading AI fiction since 2023 and know the stock moves on
sight.

The full standard is in `references/PROSE_PREFERENCES.md`. **Read all of it
before writing any prose in a session**, including section 9 (banned sentence
shapes) and section 10 (what not to overcorrect). The summary below reminds
you of it; it doesn't replace it.

## Precedence

1. The current request, and the project's own brief or canon (story
   facts, viewpoint, layout, and spelling where a project specifies it).
2. `references/PROSE_PREFERENCES.md`.
3. Generic "anti-slop" or "humanizer" guides. Where they disagree with the
   preference doc, for example on stock body language, stated feelings or
   reflective endings, the preference doc wins.

Defaults when nothing says otherwise: American spelling, past tense, close
third person.

## The core, in brief

- **A sincere narrator in the character's voice** (free indirect style). The
  narration uses the point-of-view character's vocabulary, judgments and way of
  reading things. It never stands above them to wink at the reader.
- **Performable lines.** Put tone and attitude into the sentence's shape: asides,
  interruptions, the order a character gets to the point. Don't use adverb tags.
- **Somebody to watch.** Every scene has bodies in a space, ideally someone to
  talk to. Never a character alone with records and lists for pages.
- **Concrete, lived-in texture.** Ordinary objects named precisely, each with at
  most one small human detail. Choose the telling detail and move on; texture
  is not an inventory.
- **Words for sound as well as sense.** The reader likes plain names for house and
  street things (awning, windowsill, drainpipe), wear (cracked, chipped, faded)
  and sound words (clatter, drizzle). They dislike decorative literary words
  (gossamer, ephemeral, susurrus).
- **Alternating rhythm.** Neither runs of and-chained sentences nor runs of
  short flat ones.
- **Legible feeling.** Feelings are stated plainly in the character's own terms
  and shown in the body. The best moments are when words and body pull in
  opposite directions. The narrator never interprets the scene for the reader.
- **Comedy only from a worldview played completely straight**, or from deadpan
  procedure. No zingers, banter volleys, sitcom patter, slapstick or
  "relatable" observational jokes. One wry aside in a passage at most.
- **At peak intensity, the prose breaks with the character inside a steady
  frame.** Begin composed, break, return to plain physical action.
- **Endings resonate.** Bittersweet plain thought, a suspended moment or a
  lingering image. Never uplifting abstraction, a thesis, or an ominous
  forecast.
- **Refuse the invited capper.** When a sincere line sets up a moral, a twist or
  a play on its own words, stop before it.
- **Once a line has made its point, leave it there.** No cleverer second layer
  that sells the character: a witty tail clause, a cute swap for a plain word,
  "That's not X, that's Y" in speech, "Obviously I said yes.", a character
  advertising their own trait, a narrator's wink.
- **Give the content, not a hint about the delivery.** "He said it in a tone"
  is one of the most hated lines of all. Put the actual words on the page.
- **Something to want.** Scenes are engaging because somebody wants something
  and there is something to do or discuss, not because the lines are funny.
  Restraint means not performing; dialogue still runs in full, unhurried
  thoughts.
- **Familiar words are fine; familiar tricks are not.** The banned shapes in
  section 9 include:
  - the soft doubled echo ("'Okay,' he said softly. 'Okay.'"), the worst of all
  - "It wasn't X. It was Y."
  - short sentences that overturn the one before
  - "small but real"
  - "A beat."
  - the affectionate override ("Text me anyway.")
  - narrated absence ("Nobody mentioned it.")
- **Dialogue explains itself through delivery** (v1.4). Upset or flustered
  people restart, cut off and trail off; children more than adults. Nobody,
  least of all a child, speaks in neat principles or witty retorts.
- **People push for what they want** (v1.4). They ask, demand, sulk and
  sometimes lose, rather than swallowing the want in narration.
- **Over a book, the tells are structural** (v1.4): beats between every line,
  "enough to" gauges, actions followed by their reason, every scene ending in
  calm, callbacks re-labelled, arguments settled where they began. See the
  "Book-length habits" table in section 9.
- **Leave it loose** (v1.5). The author's own hand edit loosened a model's
  clean prose almost everywhere: hedges and "Well," in speech, mid-line
  self-corrections, flustered rambling, plain colloquial afterthoughts in the
  narration, plainer words, softer wit. Economy itself reads as AI. See
  section 5, "Looseness".
- **No shorthand moments** (v1.5). A character doesn't answer an emotional
  question by citing an earlier moment as a loaded token ("She came back up
  the stairs." "What stairs?" "Never mind."). Give a brief flashback in
  narration, then a plain, fumbling line.
- **Check against real human writing, every project** (v1.6). Your idea of
  "human" is a guess with its own register: a storybook narrator ("said
  Anna", "very still", "the way you'd…", "And then") who watches from
  outside. Human fanfiction narration thinks: it asks itself questions, doubts,
  guesses "Maybe…", trails off. Teen dialogue is loud ("!", "wait", "um").
  Read real pre-2022 fic in the same fandom before drafting and measure the
  draft against it afterwards. See section 13.
- **Match the book's own gold chapters** (v1.7). When the author names some
  chapters as the standard, measure new prose against them. In the romance
  this came from, gold prose ran about 8 words a sentence and 15 words a
  paragraph, with two-"and" sentences rare (2%) and "as if" rarer still.
  New model prose drifted to 10–11 words a sentence, paragraphs of 23–30
  words, "and… and…" runs and "as if" comparisons. Looseness is short
  afterthoughts, not long sentences. See section 14.
- **The contrast cut** (v1.7). For a gut-punch memory, write the memory as
  a warm, flowing scene with literal sound, then cut hard on the page to a
  short, cold present line. Mirror the details (a butterfly on the face,
  then blood on the face) and never compare them in the narration. See
  section 7.
- **Voiced asides stay.** Never rebuild a character's own aside ("which was
  stupid") into an "X, and Y anyway" contrast; that rebuild is the AI shape.

## Working method

**Before writing:** read the reference in full. For a long piece, know each
scene's point-of-view character, what they want, and who else is in the room.

### First pass at gold standard (v1.8)

The goal is prose the author can read with no manual pass. In the past the
gap between a draft and the book's best chapters was closed by the author's
own corrections. This protocol does that work up front. Follow every step
when the book has gold chapters (or any chapter the author has praised).

1. **Load the book sheet.** Read the project's book sheet if it has one
   (for example a `STYLE-SHEET.md` at the project root). It holds each
   character's temperature and voice and what the author has approved. If
   there is none and the author has corrected drafts before, write one
   first from their notes and memory files, phrased as instructions. A
   fresh writer or a cloud session can't see your memories; the sheet is
   how the author's hand edits reach a first draft.
2. **Tune the ear.** Read in full the gold chapter nearest in time to the
   new section, and the chapter just before it. Read for the pace, the
   paragraph shapes and how each viewpoint character's narration sounds.
   Never lift lines from it, from the book sheet, or from this skill's
   examples. In testing, drafts borrowed a gold phrase, the book sheet's
   sample line and a preference-doc simile; instructions are not material.
3. **Plan every scene on scratch paper**, not in the draft:
   - one point-of-view character, the place, and the physical task or
     objects in hand;
   - what the viewpoint character wants here, and what *each* other person
     present wants, including their own take on the central relationship;
   - every turn (a decision, a confession, a piece of news) that must
     be played as spoken lines on the page, not summarised;
   - the moment where words and body pull in opposite directions;
   - how the scene ends, so endings vary across the section (see the
     first-draft failures in PROSE_PREFERENCES section 12).
4. **Draft in one pass at gold pace**: one beat per paragraph, dialogue in
   full unhurried lines, narration that thinks in the viewpoint's own words.
5. **Read your own draft against the first-draft failures list** in
   PROSE_PREFERENCES section 12 (v1.8), line by line. These are what slipped
   past the v1.7 workflow into a first draft in testing.
6. **Scanner against a gold baseline** (step 1 below, with `--baseline`).
   Then run it once more on the gold chapter you read and the draft
   together: its cross-file repeated phrases show lines you lifted from
   gold without meaning to (in testing, a draft borrowed a gold chapter's
   "face was doing several things at once"). Rebuild those.
7. **Blind gold judge.** Cut a gold excerpt of similar length at a scene
   break, and run:

   ```bash
   python3 <this-skill-dir>/scripts/gold_judge.py --out judge.md --gold GOLD_EXCERPT.md DRAFT.md
   ```

   It hides which text is gold, has a second model score both and quote
   each text's weakest lines, and says whether the draft is at the gold
   level. The judge is any OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint, set with
   `FPS_JUDGE_URL`, `FPS_JUDGE_KEY` and `FPS_JUDGE_MODEL`; a model from a
   different family than the writer works best (the testing used Gemini 4
   Argon). With no endpoint, use `--prompt-only` and give the prompt to a
   fresh subagent. Rebuild the
   quoted draft lines and anything sharing their shape; don't touch the
   rest. Judge again, with a fresh shuffle, until the judge says this
   reader would need no manual pass on the draft and its quoted weak lines
   are only small ones; stop after three rounds and tell the author what is
   still short. Don't steer by the gold text's score: it swings from run to
   run (see below). The judge also flags gold lines; ignore those. Check
   every judge flag against section 10 and the book sheet's approvals before
   acting on it: in testing it called the heroine's public cruelty
   "unsympathetic", which is exactly the contrast the author asked for, and
   it called a gold chapter's voiced asides (", which was silly") mechanical,
   though they are the author's approved voice at the author's approved
   rate. Never cut a draft below its gold chapter's own habits because the
   judge dislikes them.
   This step is not optional. In testing, a v1.8 draft that skipped it
   scored 6.5 against 9 for the best draft, on slips the judge quoted.
8. **Reviewer pass** on the final text (step 2 below), then review your own
   fixes for new tics.

In testing, the judge's blind scores put the gold chapter (9) far above an
unrevised late chapter (2.5), which the scanner's counts couldn't separate
from gold at all. But its scores are relative and lean toward whatever
ticks this document's boxes (narration questions above all). Across five
runs the same gold excerpt scored 9, 8, 5.5, 7 and 4.5, and first
drafts written to v1.7 outscored it every time they were paired.
Treat "at gold level" from the judge as a floor that catches real slips,
not as proof. The author's reading is the only proof, so read the draft
yourself too.

**Write long pieces in one continuous pass** when you can, so that callbacks
and continuity carry across sections. The reader loves callbacks, like a detail
from early on answered quietly later, as long as nothing explains them.

**Before writing, read real human writing** (v1.6). Fetch three or four
popular, complete, pre-2022 works in the same fandom or genre and read a few
chapters of each: an ordinary scene, a fight, an emotional peak. Pay most
attention to what the narration does when nothing is being said. Do this on
every project, and again before any big revision. The differences that
matter most are the ones nobody has written down yet.

```bash
python <this-skill-dir>/scripts/human_compare.py fetch --tag "Your Fandom (Source)" --count 4
python <this-skill-dir>/scripts/human_compare.py fetch --ids AO3_WORK_ID AO3_WORK_ID
```

Works published after `--before` (default 2021-12-31) are skipped, so the
corpus can't contain model prose. Text is cached locally in
`~/.cache/fiction-prose-style/human/<tag>/`; AO3 throws intermittent 525
errors, and the script retries with a wait (see the README for an optional
fetch proxy). If a new pass finds something,
add it to section 13 and the log.

**After writing, check the draft in two passes.** Each does what it is good
at: the script counts, and a reviewer reads.

**1. The scanner (counting).** Run it on the file, or on several chapters at
once to find phrases that recur across them:

```bash
python <this-skill-dir>/scripts/prose_check.py path/to/chapter.md [more.md ...]
```

It compares each section with a baseline built from the author-endorsed
chapters (`references/baseline.json`) and marks rates at least twice the
baseline with "!":
- dialogue share, sentence-length spread, and-chained sentences
- similes per 1,000 words, dashes and ellipses
- the share of paragraphs that end on a short sentence (a run of buttons)
- repeated phrases (n-grams), and pet phrases across chapters
- words used far more often than in the baseline (topic words are expected;
  look for style words)

Since v1.4 it also prints a **habits** line per chapter, because per-section
rates were too noisy. It shows:
- result-clause gauges ("enough to/that", "harder than she needed to");
- "nodded" and "shrugged";
- italic stress, for information only.

In the reader's liked chapters, "nodded" and "shrugged" occur twice in 60,000
words, and gauges run about 0.3 per 1,000 words, so a "!" there is worth
reading. The liked chapters use *more* italics than drafts usually do, so
never cut italics because of a count. Acknowledgment chains ("I know." "Yes.")
didn't separate liked prose from drafts by rate; they are a reviewer item
about placement.

It also catches fixed wordings: doubled echoes, "A beat.", "small but real",
"something shifted" and the like. These are things a model can't count
reliably over a whole chapter. A high rate is a prompt to look at the
passage, not a verdict. Rebuild what really is a habit; don't swap in
synonyms.

To rebuild the baseline from other liked prose: `prose_check.py
--build-baseline references/baseline.json LIKED.md ...` (.md with quoted or
`**Name:**` dialogue, or visual-novel `.js` scripts).

**1b. The human comparison** (v1.6). Measure the draft against the corpus:

```bash
python <this-skill-dir>/scripts/human_compare.py compare draft/*.md --corpus ~/.cache/fiction-prose-style/human/<tag>
```

For each feature it prints the range across the human works and the draft's
rate, with "!" where the draft sits well outside it. It then lists phrases
the draft uses far more than the humans (names and topic words are filtered
out), and prints a few human paragraphs that think out loud. Read those
before revising. Rows marked "(don't import)" are human habits this reader
dislikes; being below the human range there is fine. The scanner's baseline
is built from liked *model* chapters, so it can't see a model register the
liked chapters share. This comparison can.

**2. The reviewer (reading).** Start a fresh subagent with the same strong
model, and give it only `references/REVIEWER.md`, `references/PROSE_PREFERENCES.md`
and the draft. Don't give it your reasoning, the brief or earlier drafts. It
flags only the judgement shapes the script can't see (narrated absence, "It
wasn't X. It was Y.", contradiction buttons, the "selling the
characterisation" family, narrator explanation, endings, voice), quoting each
line exactly. Rebuild only the flagged lines and leave the rest alone. If a
flag is a section 10 true form on reflection, keep the line. Reviewers
overcorrect: in roleplay prompt tests, self-review cost the prose its fun, so the
reviewer is told that zero flags is a normal result.

**On long works, add a second model as a co-diagnoser** (v1.4). On a 137,000-word
novel, two models from different families (Claude and Codex) each wrote a candid diagnosis of the other's habits in
the current text, then negotiated one short working note. Each caught what the
other couldn't see in itself. Codex found the structural habits; Claude found
the sentence-level ones. Three lessons from that run:
- The **author's explicit approvals outrank the agreed note.** The reviewing
  model, following "upset speech can be fluent", stripped stammers the author
  had asked for. Restore them.
- **A different model sees different habits** (v1.6). A Gemini model,
  given the whole book, put structural patterns first: motif details
  re-recited, the same chapter-closing beat, side characters reduced to
  props. Claude reviewers mostly flagged lines. Verify its counts.
- **Review your own fixes.** The fix pass introduced new tics (deadpan
  closers, a repeated "…anyway" rebuild, cappers). The author caught one
  before the reviewer did.
- **Counts locate; they don't set targets.** No quotas. And check a count
  against the baseline before believing it: italics looked like a crutch, but
  the author's liked chapters use four times as many.

Why this split: a test on 1,626 labelled sentences showed regex patterns only
catch phrasing they were written from, and small models were right
about half the time at best on these shapes. See
`references/PROSE_PREFERENCES_LOG.md`.

**When you write a brief for another agent that will write the prose:**
- Give it `references/PROSE_PREFERENCES.md`, the canon it follows, and one
  brief file, and nothing else. In particular, not other drafts of the same
  piece.
- Phrase beats that must go unremarked as instructions to the author ("leave
  the chair's absence unmentioned"). Don't write them as prose ("nobody
  mentions it"), because authors copy a brief's wording onto the page.
- Tell it not to reuse the example sentences quoted in the reference.
- If a beat is desk work or solitary procedure, stage it in the brief with a
  second character or a physical task.
- Don't ask for charm. Words like "spark", "humor welcome", "fun", "witty" or
  "something that shows who they are" get you the self-advertising lines in
  section 9. Describe the situation instead: what each character wants, and
  what there is to do or talk about.
- For a smaller or open model (a roleplay prompt, for example), consider
  turning section 9 into plain instructions instead of passing the quoted bad
  lines along; a weaker model may imitate them. This is an untested
  hypothesis. Strong models have handled the full reference well.

**Calibrate against the book's gold chapters** (v1.7). When the author names
chapters as the standard (for example "1–25 are the gold standard"):

1. Build a baseline from them and measure every later chapter:

   ```bash
   python <this-skill-dir>/scripts/prose_check.py --build-baseline gold.json GOLD1.md GOLD2.md ...
   python <this-skill-dir>/scripts/prose_check.py --baseline gold.json LATER.md ...
   ```

   The "Pace" line compares words per sentence, words per paragraph and "as
   if" per 1,000 words. For a whole-book view, put every chapter in one
   table against the gold mean, and flag anything more than two gold
   standard deviations out.
2. Work worst-first, and start with your own new prose. In the first run
   the newly written war chapters were furthest off, and the older chapters
   had drifted less.
3. Read before changing. Split chains and long paragraphs at their joints.
   Cut "as if" unless it is funny or a character's own judgment. Leave a
   character's voice alone where it runs on in character.
4. Exempt the deliberate run-ons (a memory, a breakdown speech, the peak of
   a fight). They work because the text round them is short.
5. Re-measure, then send one reviewer pass over the revised chapters. Fixes
   breed tics: an "…anyway" overturn, a pronoun stranded after a split,
   "for a long moment" and "nodded" as filler.

**When the author sketches a scene** in their own words ("not the standard,
just the vibe"), take the *choices* from it, not the wording: who speaks,
where the mirror falls, what's visible on the page. In the first case the
author's rough sketch was better than the model's draft on four counts: the
memory was a scene with the child talking, the butterfly landed on the face,
the present-day character spoke, and the cut was visible. Keep the author's
own lines where they work, and tell them which ones you kept.

**When you judge drafts**, or set up another model to judge them, don't trust
model judges' sense of "fun", "voice" or "charm". In a judged tournament,
judges told to value literary quality rewarded flat restraint, and judges told
to value fun rewarded exactly the quips the reader hates. Use section 9, the
section 12 checklist and the author's own marked-up examples. Human
annotations outrank any judge panel.

## Tell hunt: finding exactly what reads as AI (v1.9, on demand)

Run this when someone asks for it ("run a tell hunt on X", "find what
still reads as AI"), or offer it before a big revision of the skill. It is
research for the standard, not a step in every drafting session.

The idea: hold plot, fandom and length constant, so the only difference
left between a human story and a model story is the writing. Many samples
from the same model show which tells are habits rather than one-off
choices. Two arms run side by side: **skill** (writers given
PROSE_PREFERENCES.md) finds what survives the current standard, and
**bare** (the model's own default) shows what the standard already fixes
and what it introduces.

1. **Human one-shots.** Fetch complete pre-2022 short works:

   ```bash
   python3 <this-skill-dir>/scripts/human_compare.py fetch --tag "TAG" --count 8 --min-words 1500 --max-words 5000
   python3 <this-skill-dir>/scripts/tell_hunt.py init --corpus ~/.cache/fiction-prose-style/human/<tag> --run NAME
   ```

   Defaults: 4 stories, 2 samples per story per arm, 2 pairs per judge.
   That makes 4 premise agents, 16 writers and 8 judges.
2. **Run the subagents** at every stage. Each stage prints its pending
   prompt files. Give each to a fresh subagent on the same model as the
   drafts, with the prompt "Read <prompt file> and do exactly what it
   says." and nothing else. Run them in parallel, in the background. Then
   run the next stage: `gen` after the premises, `judge` after the writers,
   `score` after the judges. `status` reprints whatever is still missing.
   - Premise agents turn each human story into neutral beats with no
     wording, so the writers never see the human text.
   - Before `gen`, read every premise. If a brief carries the story's
     phrasing or images, rewrite it as plain beats.
   - `judge` strips formatting differences (quotes, dashes, ellipses,
     scene breaks) and shuffles. No judge sees the same human story twice.
     The key stays in `key.json`.
3. **Read `report.md`.** It holds the judge's hit rate per arm, every
   quoted AI tell checked verbatim against the AI text, the human lines the
   judge mistook for AI, the human "marks" a model wouldn't write, and the
   absences. A hit rate near 50% means that arm reads as human at this
   length. That is a real result, not a failed run.
4. **Group the quotes into shapes** yourself. The judges' labels are
   freeform, so read the quotes rather than trusting the labels. Then test
   each candidate shape against both corpora:

   ```bash
   python3 <this-skill-dir>/scripts/tell_hunt.py count --run NAME 'It wasn.t \w+\. It was' '\bfor a long moment\b' --narration
   ```

   A shape is a tell when most AI stories have it and few human ones do.
   Drop it if the human stories use it about as often. Also drop anything
   the judge flagged in a human text ("Human lines the judge took for
   AI"): people write it too.
5. **Promote only what survives.** Add it to section 13 (human
   calibration) or section 9 (banned shapes), with its counts, and log the
   run in `PROSE_PREFERENCES_LOG.md`. Check each new rule against section
   10 first. A tell's fix must not ban the "true form" of a good technique.
   Absences go in as things to do, not as bans. Show the person you're working with
   the shortlist before writing it in. Their reading outranks the judges, and
   some tells may be things they like.

Don't trust a single run. Judges invent reasons when they're guessing, so
weight quotes from pairs the judge got right at high confidence. A shape
seen in one story is a choice; seen in most samples across stories, it is
a habit.
