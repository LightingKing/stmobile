# Prose preferences

Version 1.8 · 22 October 2026 · built from a ten-round blind excerpt test
and one full-chapter trial, with v1.3 additions from roleplay feedback,
v1.4 additions from a 137,000-word novel revised with two models, one
v1.5 shape I flagged while reading that novel, v1.6 calibration against
real, pre-AI human fanfiction, and v1.7 measurements of a 186,000-word
romance against the chapters I named as its gold standard, and v1.8
first-draft tests judged blind against those chapters (see
`PROSE_PREFERENCES_LOG.md` for the evidence behind the rules)

This file describes how I like fiction to be written. Give it to any model
that is writing prose for me.

**This is the master copy**, kept in the `fiction-prose-style` skill.
Projects may keep their own copy (for example `docs/PROSE_PREFERENCES.md`);
update this one first and copy it outward.

**About the examples.** Quoted lines come from one of my own stories (character
names changed) and from short test excerpts written while this document was
built. The v1.3 examples come from one roleplay reply
I marked up line by line. They illustrate principles. Never reuse them,
paraphrase them closely, or rebuild the same moments around them. Find your own.

**Precedence.** A project's own brief wins on story facts, viewpoint, layout
and spelling. This file wins over generic "anti-slop" or "humanizer" guides
wherever they disagree. Section 10 lists the places where they do.

## The short version

Write sincerely. The narrator shares the characters' world and never stands
above them to wink at the reader. In close third, the narration should sound
like the point-of-view character: their words, their judgments, their way of
reading things. I hear every sentence as it would be spoken, so neutral
narration goes dull and awkward sounds grate. Fill the page with concrete, lived-in things:
the ordinary objects of a real life, each with at most one small human detail.
Let characters feel things openly, in plain words they might use themselves,
and let the feeling show in their bodies and behaviour. The best moments are
where words and body pull in opposite directions. Never explain what a scene
means. Comedy comes only from characters sincerely living out a strange or
absurd worldview, reported deadpan. When a character reaches breaking point,
let the prose break with them inside a steady frame. End on something that
resonates, not on something that moves along. Above all, avoid the performed
sentence shapes listed in section 9. Familiar *words* are fine. Familiar
*tricks* are not. Once a line has made its point, leave it there.

Over a whole book, the tells stop being phrases and become structure: people
acknowledging each other instead of talking, narration explaining what a
gesture was for, every scene ending with everyone settled into calm, every
argument resolved where it started. Those are in section 9 too.

And check yourself against real people, every time. A model's sense of what
human prose sounds like is a guess, and the guess has a register of its own:
an old-fashioned storybook narrator who watches the character from outside.
Real fanfiction narration lives inside the character's head. It wonders,
doubts, guesses and gets things wrong. Read real human writing from the same
fandom or genre before and after drafting, and measure the draft against it
(section 13).

## 1. Register: sincere, never knowing

The narrator is inside the characters' world and takes it as seriously as they
do. It does not perform cleverness, comment on the characters from above, or
share a joke with the reader at anyone's expense.

- No asides in the general present tense ("some inheritances are voluntary").
- No "doing the thing where", no internet idiom, no fragments used as
  punchlines ("Company hair.").
- No mock-precise rankings played for a laugh ("look expensive, be
  interesting, in that order").
- No hypothetical-person comparisons that show off ("tall in the way of people
  who have not yet decided what to do about it").

The register I want to avoid has a name: *corpo-millennial*. It reads like a
2010s Tumblr post without any of the heart. Irony stands in for feeling.

**Distinguish a character's creed from narrator wisdom.** A line shaped like an
aphorism is welcome when it is the point-of-view character's sincere value,
stated from inside their world:

> She was known by whom she belonged to, and that was the whole of what she
> would have wished known.

The same shape is not welcome as the narrator handing the reader a truth about
life.

**Refuse the invited capper.** Some sentences set up an obvious follow-up: a
moral, an ironic twist, a play on the sentence's own words. I will brace for
it. Don't write it. Stop at the sincere line and trust me. Declining the easy
capper impresses me more than any amount of fine prose.

**Once a line has made its point, leave it there.** This is the same rule
applied to every line, not only sincere ones, and to the middle of a sentence
as well as the end. The common failure is a plain, real line followed by a
second, cleverer piece whose only job is to show how charming, prickly or
funny the speaker is: a witty clause tacked on, a cute swap for a plain word, a
"not X, that's Y" joke, a repeat of an answer I already have, a character
describing their own personality. Each one sells the character instead of
letting me watch them. Section 9 lists the shapes.

**Give the content, not a hint about the delivery.** When a remark matters,
tell me what was actually said. "He said it in a tone" or "she gave me a look"
in place of the words leaves me nothing to hear or stage, and it is coy: it
promises something and withholds it. Put the tone into the shape of the line
itself (see *Write lines that can be performed* below).

### Voiced narration

Because I hear what I read, I also hear the characters. **The close-third
narration should sound like the point-of-view character**: their vocabulary,
their judgments, their particular way of reading a situation. Craft books call
this *free indirect style*. Characters who "ooze personality" are why I love a
story. Clara sees one thing and interprets it her own way, and I can hear
exactly how she'd say it. From the chapter-five trial, in Agatha's section:

> She read it again for the title, which was correct in every particular, the
> article included. Whatever else might be said of the Chancellor's office, and
> Agatha could say a good deal, it had never once written a title wrong.

Nobody but Agatha would narrate that sentence. Voiced narration also
*thinks*: it wonders, doubts and guesses in the character's head instead of
only reporting what they saw (section 13). Neutral, camera-like
narration, sentences any character could have "said", is where my attention
fails. Dull sentences are hard for me to keep going through, because I have to
voice them.

**Write lines that can be performed.** I don't only hear the characters; I
stage them. Face, tone and hands play out in my head as I read. The Agatha
sentence above works because its syntax carries the delivery. The aside
("and Agatha could say a good deal") is where her tone lifts and her
eyebrows go up, and nothing had to say so. Give the inner actor something to
play: asides, interruptions, qualifications, and the order in which a
character would get to the point. Put the attitude in the shape of the
sentence rather than in a tag ("she said wryly").

**Always give me somebody to watch.** A section needs bodies in a space, and
ideally another person to talk to. A character alone at a desk working through
records, schedules and messages is the hardest thing for me to picture, even
when the details are good. When a character's idiom is procedural (a
technician who thinks in lists, a soldier who thinks in drills), let the
idiom *flavour* the narration, but still write scenes. Don't let the
narration turn into lists. Keep paragraphs of process short, break them up
with physical business, and bring in another character.

**Give them something to want.** A scene is engaging because somebody in it
wants something and there is something to do, decide or talk about with these
particular people. That is where the fun comes from, not from the lines being
funny. Characters can be playful when the situation invites it, but they don't
have to earn their place with quips.

**Understatement is fine when the situation is concrete.** A quiet, formal
character who says little is easy to picture if I know exactly where she is,
what she is looking at, and who else is in the room. Then the gaps are easy to
fill. What I can't fill is abstraction.

This is not the arch narrator. The arch narrator has a voice too, but it
belongs to the author, performing for the reader. The voice I want belongs to
the character, and it is sincere.

## 2. Texture: concrete and lived-in

Plain prose on its own is not enough. It is "a baked potato with no sour cream
and butter". The butter I want is **concrete, lived-in detail**:

> The flat was dark except for the little light over the cooker, which lit the
> kettle, the drying rack full of Jo's mismatched mugs, and the basil plant on
> the sill that neither of them remembered to water.

- Ordinary objects, named precisely, and mostly visual.
- **At most one small human detail per object**, and it implies a shared life
  without comment ("neither of them remembered to water").
- Carried in easy, list-shaped sentences. Long sentences are welcome when they
  carry *things*. They become heavy and hard to read when they carry
  *abstractions*.
- Detail filtered through a character's job or habits is good too (a nurse
  checking a plate the way she checks a patient).
- Precise, culturally specific nouns beat generic ones. "Swindon" is better
  than "a provincial town".
- Similes are acceptable as an occasional garnish. They are not the main
  texture, and they tire over length.
- **Vary the rhythm; don't live in "and".** The easy engine for long,
  textured sentences is the and-chain: "The conservatory had glass on three
  sides and forty-one storeys of air below it, and nothing had ever come in
  that way, and Ruth checked its latches morning and night." One of those in
  a paragraph is a pleasure. Paragraph after paragraph of them becomes a drone.
  Build sentences in other ways too:
  - subordinate clauses (*when*, *because*, *although*, *until*)
  - a short plain sentence
  - a sentence that opens with something other than its subject
  - a semicolon
  - a line of dialogue

  If two sentences in a row are both and-chains, rebuild one. Watch especially
  for a verdict tacked onto the end with "and" ("…and Ruth did not think much
  of it", "…and he seemed pleased"). The opposite failure, even chains of short
  declaratives, is just as monotonous. Aim for alternation, not a quota.
- **Pace: short sentences, short paragraphs (v1.7).** The chapters I named as
  a book's gold standard average about eight words a sentence. Nearly half
  their sentences are five words or fewer. Paragraphs average about fifteen
  words. Fewer than one sentence in forty carries two "and"s. A model's new
  prose drifts the other way: longer sentences, "and… and… and" runs,
  paragraphs twice as long, and many more "as if" comparisons. Each sentence
  can be fine and the page still drones. Fix the drift by splitting, not by
  rewording: one thing per sentence, a new paragraph when the beat changes.
  Save the long, run-on sentence for the moments that need it (section 7),
  where it only works *because* everything round it is short.
  - **Looseness is not length.** The padding and afterthoughts of section 5
    are short additions ("It wasn't going very well."). They are not long
    chained sentences. Gold-standard prose is both loose and short.
  - **"As if" is a simile.** "He looked at her as if he'd only just noticed
    she was there" is the commonest way a draft sneaks in a comparison. Keep
    the ones that are funny or that are a character's own judgment. Cut the
    rest, or state the plain thing.
- **Texture is not an inventory.** Choose the telling detail and give it one
  pass. Don't keep turning a small procedure over at length (weighing every
  possible phrasing of a title, logging each step of a routine) once it has
  shown who the character is. Over a long piece, lingering reads as rambling.

### Word choice

Some words make me happy. They come from recognisable families:

- **Plain names for the parts of ordinary houses and streets:** awning, stoop,
  windowsill, drainpipe.
- **Everyday materials:** linoleum, oilcloth.
- **Words for wear and use:** cracked, chipped, frayed, peeling, faded.
- **Sound words:** clatter, crunch, snap, and weather that sounds like itself
  (drizzle).

Reach for these families. Bigger words are welcome when they **name** a real
feeling, quality or smell directly: melancholy, luminous, ineffable,
petrichor. They grate when they exist mainly to sound literary: ephemeral,
gossamer, susurrus. Choose a word because it is the exact name of the thing,
never to decorate it.

**I hear every word.** I subvocalize: I "speak" what I read in my head. Sound
matters as much as sense. Drainpipe, windowsill, clatter and drizzle are
pleasing to say. "Threadbare" is not, and for me that is enough to rule it
out. Read your sentences in your head as a voice would say them. Prefer words
that are satisfying in the mouth, and rebuild sentences whose sounds collide
or clunk. This is also why monotonous rhythm (and-chains, runs of flat short
sentences) bothers me: I can hear it.

## 3. Interiority: plain feelings, in the character's own terms

Characters may say, and the narration may state, what they feel. Do it simply,
in words they might use themselves, with small self-aware human touches:

> She felt suddenly fifteen. Her hands were less steady on the milk jug than
> they'd been all week, which annoyed her.

What is **not** allowed is the narrator stepping in to interpret the scene. That
means the "he understood that…" thesis statement, a revelation of what
something "really" meant, or an aphorism that sums up the moment:

> ✗ Walter understood that he was not selling a piano at all but the last of
> the evenings June had spent at it.

The first is a person feeling something. The second is an author explaining
their own story. Once the meaning is spelled out, nothing is left for a payoff
to land on.

## 4. Feeling must be legible

I want to *see* what characters feel. Subtext that stays invisible feels like
"watching a mall camera": realistic, but cold.

- **Body and behaviour carry feeling.** A swallow, a jaw working, unsteady
  hands, her heart squeezing. Stock physical wording is acceptable (see section
  10).
- **The sweet spot is words and body pulling in opposite directions.** A
  character says "No, it's great" while everything about them shows it isn't.
- **Best of all is behaviour specific to this person, with history behind it.**
  Example: the brother who kicks his sister's tyre before her long drive, "the
  way Dad used to", and then looks embarrassed about it. It's a callback, and
  it's never explained.
- The POV character's own felt sensations are especially welcome ("Her heart
  squeezed.").

- **Composure can be the feeling (v1.7).** A character raised to keep her
  face, like the daughter of an old, strict family or a courtier, shows anger as
  overcontrol: a voice that goes quieter and more polite, something gripped
  too hard or set down too exactly, a cruelty that is precise rather than
  loud. That still has to be legible. Put the gap between the polite words
  and the gripping hand on the page, and never explain where the manner came
  from. When the composure finally fails, the break should sound
  *different*: less articulate, louder, and not clever.

## 5. Dialogue

- Restrained dialogue is good, with the feeling showing around the words
  rather than in them. Restraint means not performing; it does not mean
  short lines. Characters talk like real people: in full thoughts, at their
  own pace, sometimes several sentences at once, in ordinary language. Clipped,
  minimal exchanges read as flat (see section 10).
- Sincere, direct speech is fine and can have heart. It is less interesting
  than speech and body pulling apart.
- **No banter volleys** in which everyone is quick and clever and each exchange
  ends on a capper. It reads as a sitcom.
- **No exaggerated voice contrast**, such as the rambler paired with a
  monosyllabic foil. It reads as a cartoon. Voices should differ subtly.
- **No zingers.** A single cutting one-liner is "only funny if you're there".
- Callbacks are loved. A line late in a scene can quietly answer something set
  up earlier ("She had finished this" answering a teacher's *does not finish
  things*). It lands as the character's thought, never as a punchline.

*Added in v1.4, from my notes on a novel-length draft:*

- **Let the delivery explain the line.** I like dialogue that explains itself
  through how it's said. An upset or flustered person restarts, cuts
  themselves off, trails off: "Y-you don't understand. It's... it's
  different." That is better than a neat sentence that states the feeling.
  - Mix the shapes: a dash, an ellipsis, a restart, a wrong word corrected,
    a stammered first syllable. Don't break every line the same way.
  - Break where speech actually fails: a word the speaker doesn't want to
    say, an accusation being reformulated, an interruption. Calm speech
    stays fluent.
  - Children stumble more than adults. Let it ease as they grow up; adults
    are plainer, but can still break at their worst moments.
  - Italic stress is part of this, used where a reader would otherwise
    mis-stress the line. My liked chapters use plenty of it; don't strip it
    as a "tic".
- **No witty or moralising lines, especially from children.** A line like
  "I'm deciding whether I want to stand here listening" is too moralistic
  and too articulate for a twelve-year-old. Kids don't speak in
  principles; adults rarely should either.
- **Let conversations develop.** A chain of receipts ("I know." "Yes."
  "I will." "Good.") at an emotional moment reads as two people
  certifying each other. Let one of them go on into what they actually
  think, ask, misunderstand or need. Keep terse replies when someone is
  withholding.
- **Let characters push for what they want.** If a character wants something
  (to be included, to be told, to see someone), they should usually ask,
  demand, sulk or confront, and sometimes lose. Narrating a swallowed want
  ("She wanted to ask… but he looked tired") again and again makes a
  character feel undercooked and too nice.
- **Let hard conversations stay uneven.** A scene where someone has been hurt
  shouldn't end with both people naming the right concerns in proportionate
  language and agreeing where they stand. Leave something unanswered, or
  return to it in a later scene.

### Looseness: what my own edits do (v1.5)

I rewrote part of a model-written chapter by hand to show what I mean. The
model's version was clean and economical. Nearly every change I made loosened
it. Precision and economy are themselves a tell: every line built, nothing
spare, every sentence doing exactly one job.

- **People pad what they say.** Discourse markers and hedges: "Well,",
  "really", "I suppose", "Maybe." Afterthoughts tacked onto a line that was
  already complete ("…You'd better."). A line that would end on a neat point
  trails into something ordinary instead.
- **People correct themselves mid-line.** They say a thing, doubt it, and
  restate it: three short tries at one fact, each slightly more accurate.
- **Flustered people ramble.** A nervous yes isn't a composed "All right."
  It's several short, slightly silly attempts at an answer. A nervous question
  arrives in pieces, with the full stops in the wrong places, and often after
  a false start that the other person has to prompt ("So…" / "So?").
- **Narration takes the character's afterthoughts too.** A short, colloquial
  sentence after a finished one, in the character's own words: a "really", an
  "It wasn't going very well.", a shrug of a comment. These are not cappers.
  They have no wit in them, and they deflate rather than land.
- **Slightly imprecise, ordinary phrasing beats polished phrasing.** A
  colloquial idiom bent a little, an adverb tacked on at the end of a
  sentence, a "she was many things, but…". Prefer the plainer word ("thinking"
  over "deciding"; "laughed a bit" over "laughed despite himself").
- **Free indirect questions.** Let the narration wonder in the character's
  voice instead of reporting facts.
- **Drop the precise callback.** Narration that pins a detail to its earlier
  moment ("…the girl he had decided on before Chemistry") reads as
  constructed. Stop at "the girl he had decided on."
- **Soften the wit.** A crisp, clever refusal becomes a plain one ("Well,
  no."). A quip becomes a mild remark with a "Well." in front of it.

Loosening has its own failure modes. In the first loosened chapter a reviewer
caught three: a two-word afterthought timed as a comic button; a tail added
to a plain action to advertise the character's trait; and a self-correction
that "corrects" to the same word (the doubled echo in disguise). A real
afterthought deflates; a real correction changes something. Filler words also
pile up fast: check "really", "sort of" and "well" after a pass.

The balance: this is texture, not a quota. Don't put a "Well," on every line
or an afterthought on every paragraph. The goal is prose that sounds as if a
person wrote it without trying to make every sentence count.

## 6. Comedy

I am picky about comedy. Only two kinds work:

1. **A character's coherent, absurd worldview, lived out with total
   sincerity.** The narration reports it straight and never signals the joke.

   > "Swindon," said Mrs. Ashcombe. "Well. Somebody has to be from Swindon, and
   > I've always thought it very good of them."

   She means every word. The family "had never really recovered its
   standards, which was why they had a microwave."

2. **Deadpan procedure.** Absurd thoroughness logged flatly, item by item: a
   grandmother's typed questionnaire for the new boyfriend (full name,
   parents' occupations, whether he has ever been bankrupt, what he thinks of
   the Liberal Democrats), filed in the sideboard, followed by a very large
   slice of sponge.

Not comedy, for me: zingers, sitcom patter, slapstick, the "relatable"
observational register ("the elaborate hair she only ever bothered with for
dentists and funerals"), and gentle cuteness. Warmth is welcome as texture, but
it is not the joke.

**Wit tolerance is low.** One wry aside in a passage ("which she was going to
eat herself later, out of spite") is the ceiling.

## 7. Intensity: when things fall apart

At a character's worst, **the prose should break with them, inside a steady
frame.**

1. Begin composed, in the normal voice.
2. Break: turn on the absent or offending person as *you*, run on through a
   long breathless sentence, and drop to single-line paragraphs.
3. Return to plain physical action at the end. The ordinary world carrying on
   is part of what makes it hurt.

> He turned the reel until it clicked free. Through the kitchen window his
> mother called his name, and he put the rod back on its bracket, and it didn't
> sit straight, and he left it.

At peak intensity, the **voice** breaking outperforms heavy bodily description.
Body language carries mid-level feeling. Past a certain point, the prose itself
has to crack.

### The contrast cut (v1.7)

One of the hardest-hitting moments I've asked for works like an anime
flashback. A warm, almost overexposed memory, with music in it, cuts hard to
the same person, cold and bloody, in the present. The contrast should make
you want to curl over. In prose:

- **The memory is a scene, not a summary.** Somebody small talks, rambles,
  promises, begs. The reader has to watch a person for the cut to cost
  anything.
- **The memory is the one place for the long, flowing sentence**, full of
  light, colour and *literal* sound. Use an object already in the story,
  like a music box playing on the terrace, not an imagined soundtrack.
  Keep the detail concrete: summer, not haze.
- **The cut is visible on the page.** A section break, or a hard paragraph
  break, with no transition word. Never write "the memory faded" or "and
  then she was back".
- **The present starts on a short, cold, concrete sentence.** The music is
  gone, so there is silence, or only breathing.
- **The two halves mirror each other, and the narration never compares
  them.** Running toward her against standing still. "Look!" against saying
  nothing. Something alive held carefully in an open hand against a blade
  gripped in a fist. Where possible, put the mirror in the same place: a
  butterfly on the face, then blood on the face. The reader does the
  matching.
- **Keep the frame.** Let the point-of-view character see the person from a
  little way off first. In the memory the child looks up at her; in the
  present, nobody looks at her at all.

## 8. Endings

End on something that **resonates**, not something that moves along. Endings
that merely continue the action or trade another line of dialogue are the
weakest.

Good endings:
- A character's own plain, **bittersweet** reflection, built from concrete
  details already in the scene. Frank will remember "the pink helmet and the
  streamers and her elbows", she probably won't, "and he found he didn't mind
  at all".
- A **suspended moment** cut short ("She looked back, and saw him, and her
  mouth opened.").
- A **lingering image** (his arms still bent round a saddle that isn't there).
- A modest, practical close ("For now, that would have to do.").

Bad endings: abstract, uplifting or mystical closure ("And somehow, that was
enough"), thesis statements, forecasts, and ominous cutaways.

## 9. Banned sentence shapes (the core list)

**Familiar words are fine. Familiar rhetorical tricks are not.** Nearly
everything I hate is a small performed two-step that calls attention to
itself. Do not swap in synonyms to get around these, because the shape itself
is the problem.

| Shape | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Soft doubled echo** (the worst) | "'Okay,' he said softly. 'Okay.'" |
| Negation-reversal | "It wasn't anger. It was something quieter, and worse." · "not the café itself, but…" |
| Echo-denial | "He said it like it was nothing. It wasn't nothing." |
| Contradiction button | "'I'm fine.' She wasn't." · "He tried for a grin. It didn't reach his eyes." (as a separate capping sentence) |
| Qualifier flip | "She smiled, small but real." |
| Screenplay timing | "A beat. Then: 'Okay.'" |
| Banter capper | "'You're impossible.' / 'You love me.' / 'Unfortunately.'" |
| Comic button | Narration repeats a line and caps it with an idiom: "Very good. She would take very good." |
| Affectionate override | A practical objection met with a stubborn repeat that means "I love you": "'It's three hours.' 'Text me anyway.'" · "'You didn't have to.' 'I wanted to.'" |
| Thesis or uplifting closure | "And somehow, that was enough." · "Home, she thought. Maybe this was what home felt like." |
| Personified abstraction | "The silence stretched between them." |
| Cute exaggeration summing up a person | "the lemon Jo squeezed over *everything*" · "only ever bothered with for dentists and funerals" |

**Narrated absence** needs its own warning, because it survives every other
filter. This means describing what *didn't* happen or *wasn't* said:
"Ruth did not mention it", "None of them mentioned the chair, and Ines did
not ask", "Nobody came within three metres", "Nobody told Ines any of this."
If something goes unremarked, just don't remark on it. Naming the silence
breaks it. A short "Nobody…" sentence closing a paragraph is especially
tic-like. One pointed absence per chapter can earn its place; a dozen cannot.
(When you write a brief, phrase it as "leave the chair's absence unmentioned",
not "nobody mentions it". Models copy the brief's wording onto the page.)

Softer dislikes (avoid, but not fatal): "Something shifted between them.",
"The words hung in the air.", "He didn't say anything. He didn't have to.",
and "because that was what you did".

**Canary words** (2026-10-20): "stay" / "stayed", "anyway(s)" and "home" often
mark an AI-ism, though not always. Read each one in context. The bad forms are
a body part or person left lingering ("her hand stayed at his temple",
"…and stayed there."), the narrative "X, and Y anyway" overturn, and a
sentimental "home" ("I just wanted you home"). Literal uses and ordinary
speech are fine. Rebuild the sentence rather than swapping in a synonym.

### Selling the characterisation (added in v1.3)

These are the add-on layers from section 1: a line makes its point, then
something cleverer is attached to show how charming the speaker is. They are
just as common in dialogue as in narration, and the negation-reversal in the
table above is banned in speech too. In the sample these come from,
the coy hint was the line I hated most.

| Shape | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Coy hint instead of content** (one of the worst) | "and he said it in a tone" · "she gave me a look" · "the way he said it" · casual vague labels ("the lentil thing") |
| Witty tail clause | A plain line, then a joke attached with a comma or "and": "…it picks up the room tone, and the room tone in here is the fridge." |
| Cute figurative swap | A cute phrase in place of the plain one: "Shut that, you're letting the hallway in." |
| Spoken "not X, that's Y" | "That's not a set, that's a hostage situation." |
| Redundant confirmation | Repeating an answer the reader already has, as a button: "So I said yes. Obviously I said yes." |
| Self-advertising line | A character naming their own trait or performing it at the reader: "so I have an excuse to be unbearable about it" |
| Narrator wink | A wry verdict attached to a description: "…one drill squashed flat, and she'd clearly given up on fixing it." |

The fix is to stop at the plain line, or to replace a hint with what was
actually said. Don't swap in a different joke.

### Book-length habits (added in v1.4)

These are invisible in a paragraph and obvious across a book. The stock
phrases in the tables above were already gone from the novel where these were
found; these structural habits were not. Rates are measured against my liked
chapters (the scanner's baseline).

| Habit | What it looks like | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stage-direction metronome** | line / *she nodded* / line / *he looked at her* / line. My liked chapters use "nodded" zero times; the novel used it about once per 1,200 words. | A beat between lines must show something specific (an object, a task, a look that means something), or it goes. Let lines run back to back. |
| **Result-clause gauge** | "pleased enough to…", "cold enough that…", "harder than she needed to". It measures every feeling against a consequence. | State the action, or the consequence on its own. |
| **Stating an action's emotional job** | "…for something to do with her hands", "…so he could choose", a gesture followed by why the character did it. Also a trailing "-ing" clause that interprets: "…, reminding them that…". | Let the action stand. Explain only when the reason changes what the reader understands. |
| **Arranging everyone into calm** | Scene after scene ending on hands, warmth, a head on a shoulder, breathing slowing. | Keep the strongest such endings. Elsewhere end on a particular consequence, an open question or a changed task. |
| **Labelled callbacks** | An old object returns with its history recited ("the studs she had bought at fourteen and given him at fifteen…"). | Let recognition do the work. Bring the object back in use. |
| **Shorthand moment** (v1.5) | A character cites an earlier moment as a loaded token that carries the meaning for them. Asked "Does she even like you?", he answers "She came back up the stairs." / "What stairs?" / "Never mind." It is knowing and coy: the speaker talks like an author pointing at a symbol, and the cryptic reply invites the reader to admire the reference. The same move in narration is a one-line paragraph that only names the moment ("He thought of the stairs."). | If the character has to think of the moment, give a brief flashback in narration: what they actually saw and heard, in a sentence or two. Then let them answer in plain, fumbling words that don't know what the moment means ("She's... well, she's nice sometimes."). |
| **Briefings and summarised turns** | Plans and revelations delivered as tidy exposition; an important reconciliation reported in a paragraph of indirect speech. | Give the turn a scene, or at least two real lines. Compress routine, not feeling. |
| **Tidy settlement** | Children separating affection from blame in complete, proportionate language; a scene ending on a status report ("we're fine for today"). | Let people be particular and uneven. Don't manufacture unfairness either. |
| **Deadpan closer clusters** | A wry, observational last line (the shelter "with a roof and no walls"). One is charming; six in twenty chapters is a tic. | Keep the best one or two. |
| **Detachable meanness** | A character's cruelty reported in a sentence that could be moved to any scene ("she told him to stop, because…"). | Play it: give the swipe a target and a reply, inside the encounter. |
| **Adult diction in a child's point of view** | "apparently satisfied", "ostensibly", "acutely aware" in an eleven-year-old's section. | Use the words that child would use. Formal is fine where it *is* the character. |
| **Triads** | "hungry, cross, and ready to fly". | Two things, or one. |

## 10. Do not overcorrect

Most of the patterns I dislike are good techniques used without restraint.
Each has a true form: used once, literally, from inside a character's way of
thinking, and then left alone. For example, Agatha choosing a phrasing for
a message to the Duchess's court:

> *Has been pleased to name* was better; it let the court see Clara's face while
> she did it.

That sentence explains a choice, but in plain functional terms and in the
courtier's own logic, with no simile or moral after it. Aim for the true form;
don't delete the technique.

Generic anti-AI guides will tell you to remove some things I actually like.
**Keep these:**

- **Stock physical and emotional wording**, used as plain statements: "barely
  above a whisper", "her eyes stung", "she pressed her lips together", "her
  chest ached with something she couldn't name", "the kind of cold that gets
  into your bones", "her heart squeezed", "his jaw worked", even "his smile
  didn't quite reach his eyes" when it is one plain sentence and not a
  capping button.
- **Visible body language in general.** More visible feeling beats less.
- **Stated feelings**, from inside the character.
- **Short exasperated thought** in the character's voice ("Of course he did.").
- **Occasional similes.**
- **Modest reflective endings** (section 8).
- **Long sentences**, when they carry concrete things.
- **Habitual words used literally.** "Sat" is style when a physical thing
  actually sits somewhere ("a box of baubles sat in the corner"). It is a tic
  when an abstraction sits ("the question sat between them"). The same goes for
  "hung", "settled", "held" and similar words.

Also do not overcorrect into bare minimalism. Stripped, flat prose ("The girl
was young. She asked to play.") is actively disliked.

Two guards for the v1.3 rules:

- **Asides that carry the character's own judgment stay.** The Agatha
  aside in section 1 ("and Agatha could say a good deal") is attached to
  a line too, but it is her sincere opinion and it changes how the sentence is
  delivered. An add-on from section 9 is a joke for the reader's benefit. Ask
  whose voice the extra piece is in, and whether it would survive if nobody
  were watching.
- **Voiced asides are not tics (v1.4).** "Her heart jumped, which was
  stupid; it was one finger." is the character's own judgment, and I like
  it. Rebuilt as "It was only one finger, and her heart had jumped anyway."
  it is worse, and more AI-like: the "…anyway" / "…instead" overturn is a
  stock shape of its own. Cut an aside only when it has become a repeated
  habit. Never re-engineer it into a contrast.
- **What I've approved outranks a general rule (v1.4).** A reviewing model
  told that "upset speech can be fluent" removed stammers I had asked for.
  When I've explicitly approved a technique, keep it.
- **Counts find problems; they don't set targets (v1.4).** Cutting a habit
  to a quota swaps it for a new one. Read the passage the number points to.
- **Fixes breed new tics (v1.4).** A revision pass introduces its own
  repeated shapes: new cappers, new deadpan closers, the same rebuild used
  ten times. Review your own fixes as hard as the original.
- **Don't let "leave it there" make dialogue clipped.** A roleplay preset
  built on an earlier version of this file produced dialogue I found too
  restrained and flat. I want full conversational exchanges, sincere and
  unhurried. Cut the clever second layer, not the conversation.

## 11. Defaults

- **Spelling:** American, unless the project says otherwise.
- **Tense:** past.
- **Person:** close third by default. First person is welcome if it's done well.

## 12. Before you return the prose

1. Search for the section 9 shapes, especially doubled echoes, "It wasn't X.
   It was Y.", and short sentences that overturn the one before. Rebuild the
   passage; do not reword it. Also look for sentences that follow a sincere
   line only to cap it with a moral or a twist. Cut the capper.
2. Check that every scene has concrete, lived-in detail on the page, and that
   the rhythm alternates: no run of and-chained sentences, and no run of
   short flat ones.
3. Check that feelings are visible, and that no narrator sentence explains what
   a moment means. Check that every section has bodies in a space to watch,
   and that the narration sounds like its point-of-view character.
4. Check that no character is quipping, and that any comedy is a worldview
   played straight.
5. Check the last paragraph of every scene. Does it resonate, or does it only
   move along or sum up?
6. Read each line of dialogue and each description for a second layer. Does
   it add a cleverer piece after making its point: a tail joke, a cute word
   swap, a repeat, a character advertising themselves, a narrator's wink? Cut
   it. Where a remark matters, check that its actual words are on the page and
   not only a hint at its tone.
7. Over a long piece, check the book-length habits in section 9: beats
   between lines, result-clause gauges, actions explained, scenes all ending
   in calm, callbacks re-labelled, arguments settled where they began, and
   past moments cited as knowing shorthand in dialogue. Check that the prose
   isn't too economical: people pad, hedge and correct themselves, and the
   narration has some of the character's loose afterthoughts (section 5,
   "Looseness"). Check
   that characters ask for what they want, and that upset dialogue breaks
   the way people actually break.
8. If you are working with the skill's tools, run the scanner for the counts
   and then the reviewer pass (see the skill's working method).
9. Compare the draft with real human writing from the same fandom or genre
   (section 13). Read some of it first, then measure. Look hardest at
   whether the narration thinks along with the character or only watches.
10. If the book has chapters I've named as its standard, measure the draft
    against those (section 14): sentence length, paragraph length,
    "and"-chains, "as if". Split what has drifted, except where a run-on is
    doing a job. Then have a second model compare the draft with a gold
    excerpt blind (the skill's `gold_judge.py`).
11. Read a first draft for the first-draft failures below.

### First-draft failures (v1.8)

Model first drafts written to this standard came close to my gold
chapters, and a blind judge sometimes ranked them above gold. What still
needed a hand was a short list of slips, and they tend to arrive together.
Read a first draft for these before anything else:

- **Broken mechanics from your own edits.** Two quotations by one speaker
  fused on a line (`…knows.” “And she's…`), a stray or missing quotation
  mark, a pronoun left pointing at the wrong person after a split. Check
  every line you touched.
- **A delivery gloss after real words.** The line is on the page, then the
  narrator labels how it was said: "She said it flatly, the way she read
  out recipe instructions." Let the line and the body carry it.
- **Wisdom credited to a character.** An aphorism passed through reported
  speech so the narrator can say it: "Length, he said, was for people who
  expected an argument." It is still narrator wisdom.
- **Doubled emphasis.** A sentence restated more strongly: "She hadn't
  thought of that. She hadn't thought of it at all." The same goes for the
  spoken echo-denial: "Nothing." / "It's not nothing."
- **Clever detection.** A side character proves they knew all along with
  one neat deduction ("You always put your polish out on Fridays."). Once
  in a chapter is fine; twice is the narrator showing off the character.
- **Too many quotable last lines.** Each scene ends on a crafted button:
  a dry order, a deadpan reply, a callback that restarts something. One or
  two in a chapter are a pleasure. Let other scenes stop on a plain action
  or mid-motion.
- **Model verb clusters.** "came out of", "came down", "went up" for
  everything (section 13). Use the specific verb.
- **The book's own known fix-tics.** Every book collects lines the author
  has had to strip more than once (in one book: "Somebody had to…",
  "that's not the same", "didn't know what to do with that"). Keep them in
  the book sheet and search the draft for them.
- **Side characters who serve.** A friend appears only to hand over a
  clue, an object or an absolution and then vanishes. Each person in a
  scene has their own want, and pushes for it.
- **Summarised turns.** The article, the letter, the argument, the
  apology: if it changes something, it goes on the page as words.

## 13. Calibrate against real human writing (v1.6)

Everything above was learned from my reactions to model prose. That teaches
a model what to avoid, but not what people actually do, and the gap is easy
to miss because the model fills it with its own idea of "human". This
section exists because a 92,000-word novel passed every earlier check and
was still plainly unlike the fanfiction it was meant to sit beside.

**Do this constantly, not once.** On every project, before drafting, read a
few chapters of real human writing in the same fandom or genre. After
drafting, measure the draft against it. A note like this one is no
substitute: the differences that matter most are the ones nobody thought to
write down. Use works published before 2022, so none of them can be
model-written. Popular, complete works on AO3 sorted by kudos are a good
pool. Use at least three or four authors, because single writers vary a lot.

**What the first comparison found.** Four popular fics from one cartoon fandom
(2015–2020, about 220,000 words, four authors) against a model-written
novel in the same fandom that had already been through the v1.5 review.
Rates per 1,000 words:

| Feature | Human range | Model draft |
|---|---|---|
| Questions in the narration (outside dialogue) | 0.7–2.7 | 0.02 |
| Ellipses | 2.1–4.8 | 0.05 |
| "!" | 2.0–9.1 | 0.8 |
| "?" | 6.2–12.4 | 3.4 |
| "said Anna" order (verb before name) | 0–0.5 | 4.9 |
| "very" | 0.6–1.3 | 4.5 |
| "the way…" comparisons ("the way you'd hold a pencil") | 0.3–0.6 | 3.2 |
| Sentences starting with "And" | 1.2–2.5 | 4.1 |

Phrases with no human match at all in 220,000 words included "came out of"
(36 times in the draft), "came down" (67 against 1), "very quietly" (26),
"she felt it" (45), "the way she'd" (31) and "and she didn't" (27).

What those numbers add up to:

- **The narrator thinks, not watches.** This is the biggest difference. Human
  narration in close third is the character's mind running: it asks itself
  questions ("Where was she supposed to go from here?"), doubts ("Was she
  being paranoid? Perhaps."), speculates in strings of "Maybe…", gets
  distracted, gets things wrong, catches itself ("Why was that? Ah."), and
  trails off mid-thought ("The barn had been…"). The model narrator reported
  what the character did and saw, in a calm, even voice, and almost never
  wondered anything. Free indirect style (section 1) means this. Being in
  the character's vocabulary is not enough if the narration never thinks in
  it.
- **The storybook register is a model fingerprint.** "said Anna", "very
  still", "very quietly", "the way you'd…", "And then…", "came down", "went
  up and up": together they make a British children's-classic narrator. It
  is consistent and pleasant, and no fanfic writer in the sample sounds like
  it. Write "Anna said" or drop the tag; use "very" and "the way…" rarely;
  don't start sentences with "And" as a cadence.
- **Teenagers sound like teenagers.** Human dialogue for young characters is
  full of "!", "?", "wait", "um", "like", "okay so", sentences that go up at
  the end ("Kind of?"), and emphatic italics. It is louder and messier than a
  model's well-behaved speech, even at calm moments.
- **Peaks go inward.** At an emotional peak, human writers move deeper into
  the head: italic thought lines, repetition ("*I ruined it. I ruined it.*"),
  runs of "She wanted…", half-finished sentences. The model draft stayed on
  the outside of the body and let one gesture carry everything. Both can
  work, but a whole book of the outside view reads as restraint performed.
- **The narrator is allowed attitude and digression.** Parenthetical asides,
  a train of thought chased for a sentence or two, a little exasperation,
  ordinary modern things (phones, a chain restaurant, a therapist's name).

**Don't import the human habits I dislike.** Real fanfiction also has
said+adverb tags ("said dryly"), said-bookisms ("replied", "exclaimed",
"declared"), epithets for names ("the smaller gem"), nodding and smiling on
every page, and tense slips. They are common, but they make prose read as
rough, not as human. Sections 1 and 9 still hold. The aim is the thinking,
loose, noisy voice, not the mistakes.

**Loosening toward human voice breeds its own habits.** In the first full
rewrite, the new free indirect questions, delivery glosses and "looked at
her for a long time" pauses all became tics within five chapters. After
any voice rewrite, run the reviewer pass and look for a new move that
repeats in the same words, not only for the old shapes.

**The tool.** `scripts/human_compare.py` (see the skill's working method)
fetches pre-2022 works into a local cache and prints the human range for
each feature next to the draft's rate, the phrases the draft uses far more
than the humans do, and a few human paragraphs that think out loud, to read
before revising. As with the scanner, a "!" says where to look, not what
number to hit.

## 14. Calibrate against my gold chapters (v1.7)

When I name some chapters of a book as the standard ("1–25 are the gold
standard"), measure every later chapter against them, not against general
ideas of good prose. In the 186,000-word romance this came from, the gold
chapters measured:

| Measure | Gold chapters | Typical drift in new model prose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Words per sentence | about 8 | 10–11 |
| Sentences of 5 words or fewer | about 46% | under 40% |
| Words per paragraph | about 15 | 23–30 |
| Sentences with two or more "and"s | about 2% | 8–13% |
| "As if / as though" per 1,000 words | about 0.4 | 1–2.8 |
| Dialogue share | about 26% | 14–20% in action and aftermath |

What I'm asking for:
- **Measure first, then read.** The numbers find the chapters and passages
  that drifted. Reading decides what to change. Some long chapters are long
  because a character's voice runs on, like a gossip's interlude or a
  haggle, and they read right. Leave those.
- **Split, don't reword.** Break the chain at its natural joints. Give each
  action its own sentence. Start a new paragraph when the beat or the
  speaker changes.
- **Protect the deliberate run-ons:** a warm memory, a breakdown speech, the
  break at peak intensity (section 7). They work by contrast with the clipped
  text round them. The scene as a whole will still measure long, and that's
  fine.
- **Review your own fixes.** Splitting sentences breeds its own tics: a
  stray "X, and Y anyway"; a pronoun that now points at the wrong person
  because the sentence that named them was cut; "for a long moment" and
  "nodded" creeping back in as filler beats. Check each one.

## Open questions

These have not yet been tested at length:
- Whether similes cloy across a whole chapter.
- Whether stock body language wears thin when it is on every page.
- Whether the findings hold in American settings (the test excerpts leaned
  British). Partly answered in v1.6: the British storybook cadence is itself
  a model tell in American fandoms (section 13).
- Section 13 rests on one fandom (a cartoon fandom, four works). Repeat it
  for each new fandom or genre, and add what it finds.
- The v1.3 rules come from one marked-up roleplay reply and from tuning
  roleplay dialogue, not from the blind test. They have not yet been checked
  on chapter-length fiction.
- Whether quoting bad examples, as this file does, makes weaker models imitate
  them. Strong models have handled it well (see the skill's working method).
- The v1.7 gold-chapter numbers come from one book (a fantasy romance
  fanfic, close third, British-set). Other books' gold chapters may run at a
  different pace. Measure each book's own.
- The v1.4 book-length habits come from one novel (two models' layers, one
  author's notes). The rates are measured against my liked chapters, but
  only "nodded", the result clauses and "I know" have been counted that way.
